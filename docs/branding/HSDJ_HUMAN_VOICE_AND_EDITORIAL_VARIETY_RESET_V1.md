# HSDJ human voice and editorial variety reset

**Date:** 2026-09-17  
**Status:** Research and proposed copy/composition. Not approved production copy.  
**Creative trigger:** Patrick rejected the unattributed Weddings “best dance floors” sentence and the repeated equal-card grid shown on About.  
**Production boundary:** The frozen anti-template protocol prohibits production edits before a mission-specific `PATRICK_CREATIVE_APPROVAL: GRANTED` marker. This document prepares decisions; it does not grant that marker.

## The problem is not grammar

The site often says true-enough things in a voice no working DJ would use: every sentence rounded off, every room “flowing,” every night “unfolding,” every detail tied up in a neat three-part promise. Worse, it repeats the same visual wrapper. A visitor sees several equal cards with a small neon label, a white headline, a grey paragraph, and the same mixer collage. Even interesting facts become skimmable because every block has the same weight and shape.

Patrick's answers in `Q's For HSDJ.txt` contain the counterweight: five-hour Spotify playlists are welcome; he may play around two-and-a-half minutes of many songs; dinner should lead toward the dance party; before a speech he checks the coordinator, camera crew, wireless signal, and speaker; requests can reveal songs that mean something to the group; the floor can wake up for a left-field rock track. These details have varying scale and rhythm. The layout should too.

## Immediate editorial decisions proposed

### Weddings photograph caption / faux quote

**Current:** “The best dance floors don’t feel forced. They unfold naturally: the right song at the right moment, your people fully present, and a room that slowly lets go together.”

**Decision:** Delete it. It is an unattributed `<blockquote>` containing no verifiable observation, action, or useful decision. Do not replace it with another universal-sounding dance-floor aphorism.

**Candidate caption, in Patrick's voice, based on answer 3:** “Sometimes it’s a rock song from left field. Heads turn, and people come running onto the floor.”

This is a draft paraphrase of Patrick's answer, **not** a direct quotation or a specific documented wedding. Patrick should approve or rephrase it. A stronger alternative is to use no caption and let the actual crowd photograph and a separately attributed couple review carry the section.

### About credibility grid in Patrick's screenshot

The four equal cards should not be rewritten as four better equal cards. Retire the grid. Preserve the useful content but give it different editorial forms:

1. **A concise credential line:** OIART graduation and production work are factual claims currently on the site. Verify exact wording and attribution before amplifying them. Do not conclude with “a mix that is built, not guessed.”
2. **A large “Before someone gives a speech” sequence:** Coordinator okay; photographer/videographer ready; wireless mic signal checked; speaker ready. This comes directly from Patrick's answer 5. It shows competence without the generic promise “audio you can trust.”
3. **A small live-set observation:** Listen for requests, cheering, laughter, and whether the room is too loud, too quiet, or out of balance. Answers 2, 3, and 7 provide the source. Do not turn it into another “reading the room” paragraph.
4. **A real couple's words, visually distinct:** Use one source-checked review with name and context instead of “People often say...” and a marketer's paraphrase. Cassandra's published review contains “the dance floor was packed at all times!”; Stephen speaks to Patrick's care. Neither needs a summary underneath explaining what it proves.

The visual studies in `design-research/hsdj-site-voice-layout-v1/` explore two different compositions for this one About section. Their purpose is to test hierarchy and stopping power, not to create a reusable new grid template.

This is a high-impact triage, not an assertion that every sentence matching “energy” or “flow” is bad. Those words are allowed when attached to a specific musical or technical observation. The test is whether deleting the sentence would cost the visitor a fact, a useful distinction, a real human moment, or a decision.

## How to make long-form SEO content worth reading

SEO copy should be the answer to a real question, not filler needed to reach a word count. Each page should contain at least one detail a couple could repeat to someone else after a quick scan. Examples of possible stopping points from Patrick's own source answers:

- “A five-hour playlist is not too much.” Then explain how a playlist guides a live set.
- “Before a speech, I check four things.” Then list the real checks.
- “A request can tell me more about the room than a genre label.” Then explain when Patrick uses or declines one.
- “Dinner music should not put everybody to sleep.” Then explain Patrick's preference for upbeat classics and a gradual build, without promising a fixed BPM formula.
- “Outdoor ceremonies need a power plan.” Then discuss verified equipment/venue arrangements without claiming every venue has the same issue.

These lines are editorial starting points, not approved site copy. Avoid making every section a punchy one-liner. Let some be a direct answer, some an image with a precise caption, some a short piece of evidence, and some a longer useful explanation.

## Visual-variety rule

Do not solve the repeated-grid problem by inventing a new card skin and using it everywhere. Match form to evidence:

- A checklist or sequence for a real sequence of work.
- A large, attributed quote when a couple's voice is the evidence.
- A comparison only when there are choices to compare.
- A single image and short caption when the image is genuinely the point.
- A narrow technical note for a credential, specification, or source fact.
- Open editorial text where no container is needed.

On a page, vary width, density, text length, colour use, image crop, and empty space deliberately. Avoid four equal columns or four equal tiles unless four options truly have equal decision weight. Decorative mixer artwork must not make separate content items look identical.

## Acceptance test before any line or section ships

1. Can Patrick say the line out loud in a consult without feeling he is reading an ad?
2. Which of his answers, a verified source, a review, or a documented service fact supports it?
3. Does it tell the visitor something they did not already learn on this page?
4. Is a metaphor hiding a missing fact?
5. Does the layout help this fact stand out, or bury it in another equal card?
6. If removed, does the visitor lose useful information or a reason to act? If not, cut it.
7. Is a quote truly attributable? If it is Patrick's paraphrased view, do not format it as a client testimonial.
