# Howe Sound Wedding DJ Voice and Website Copy Standard V2

**Status:** Draft governance for Patrick's review  
**Production copy:** Not approved until Patrick signs the approval line at the end  
**Owner:** Patrick Mallan  
**Applies to:** Website copy, metadata, navigation, forms, venue pages, guides, stories, captions, calls to action, and future campaign writing  
**Supersedes on approval:** `HSDJ_DJ_VOICE_DIRECTION_V1.md`  
**Works under:** `HSDJ_ANTI_TEMPLATE_DESIGN_EXECUTION_PROTOCOL_V1.md` and `HSDJ_CREATIVE_CONSTITUTION_V1_1.md`

## Why this version exists

V1 found the right direction. Patrick should sound like a working DJ with taste, judgment, local knowledge, and enough experience to know when not to interfere.

The website now needs more than a direction. It needs a writing standard that can stop technically correct copy from becoming generic, overworked, or obviously generated.

The problem is not grammar. The problem is language that has been polished until nobody talks that way.

Common warning signs already present in the site's wider copy system include:

- the same small group of words doing too much work: *flow, pacing, arc, atmosphere, energy, seamless, intentional,* and *premium*;
- three-part headlines that sound composed for a pitch deck;
- complete, balanced sentences in places where a person would be shorter and plainer;
- claims about weddings that are not tied to a real moment, action, venue, or consequence;
- strategic language presented to couples as if it were customer language;
- a DJ trying to sound like a luxury agency;
- every paragraph ending with a polished conclusion;
- different pages saying roughly the same thing in different words.

This standard is here to remove those habits.

## The central rule

Write what Patrick knows, notices, does, asks, changes, fixes, or leaves alone.

Do not begin with the kind of sentence a wedding company is expected to publish. Begin with the actual thing that happens.

Examples:

- The microphone is checked before the speeches begin.
- Patrick asks which version of the first-dance song the couple wants.
- Dinner music should not fight the conversation.
- A packed dance floor does not need a speech from the DJ between songs.
- Some rooms are ready for drum & bass at 11:30. Some are not.
- If rain moves a ceremony indoors, the sound plan changes with it.

These are examples of the required level of reality. They are not automatically approved website copy.

## Who is speaking

The website does not need one voice everywhere. It needs four related voices with clear jobs.

### 1. Patrick speaking

Use first person when the copy expresses Patrick's taste, experience, promise, opinion, or way of working.

It should sound like something he could say during a consultation without feeling as though he is reading an advertisement.

Good territory:

- what he listens for;
- what he asks couples;
- what he prepares;
- what he has learned from actual rooms;
- what he likes about DJing weddings;
- what he will and will not do;
- an honest opinion that helps a couple make a decision.

Patrick's voice can be warm, funny, blunt, enthusiastic, or matter-of-fact. It should not be turned into a character who speaks in slogans all day.

### 2. Howe Sound Wedding DJ speaking

Use the company voice for page introductions, service explanations, navigation, and factual statements.

This voice should be clear and lightly distinctive. It does not need to imitate Patrick in every sentence. Its job is to help somebody understand the offer and choose what to do next.

### 3. The interface speaking

Buttons, form instructions, availability messages, errors, pricing labels, and menus should be plain.

Use:

- Check your date
- Book a consult
- See packages
- Read the reviews
- Tell me about your wedding
- That email address does not look right

Do not turn small interface messages into brand theatre. A submit button is not a headline.

### 4. Couples speaking

Reviews belong to the couple who wrote them.

- Preserve their wording.
- Do not make a quote more dramatic by removing the reason behind it.
- Keep the couple's name and useful wedding or venue context where permission allows.
- Never combine fragments from different reviews.
- Do not make spelling or grammar edits that alter personality or meaning.
- A short quote can stay short. It does not need a marketing caption underneath explaining what it proves.

## What Patrick should sound like

Patrick has loaded equipment through service entrances, dealt with weather changes, checked microphones, listened to requests, watched grandparents dance, and worked out when a room wants another familiar song instead of a clever choice.

The writing should carry that experience without constantly announcing it.

The voice is:

- conversational;
- specific;
- music-literate without showing off;
- direct;
- prepared;
- playful when the moment allows it;
- comfortable having an opinion;
- local when there is something real to say;
- imperfect enough to sound spoken;
- confident without pretending every decision is profound.

The voice is not:

- corporate;
- luxury-coded;
- reverent about ordinary service tasks;
- a nightclub promoter pasted onto a wedding website;
- a wedding planner;
- a poet narrating every transition;
- a marketing strategist speaking directly to customers;
- a collection of DJ metaphors;
- deliberately rough, slang-heavy, or careless to prove it is human.

## Human does not mean badly written

Removing the AI sound does not mean adding fake typos, awkward slang, random fragments, or unnecessary swearing.

Human writing has judgment. It knows when the plain sentence is the best sentence.

Use these behaviours:

- Put short sentences beside longer ones.
- Use contractions.
- Let a paragraph end without a grand conclusion.
- Allow one useful thought to occupy a sentence by itself.
- Vary paragraph size naturally.
- Use an occasional fragment for emphasis, not as a system.
- Keep a slightly unusual phrase when it is something Patrick would really say.
- Prefer a real noun and verb to an adjective and abstraction.
- Use humour only when the observation is genuinely funny.
- Stop once the point is made.

Avoid these behaviours:

- making every sentence the same length;
- forcing three benefits into one line;
- writing every heading as a campaign slogan;
- repeating the same rhetorical pattern across a page;
- ending every section with a soft sales promise;
- manufacturing quirks to simulate personality;
- writing in fragments simply because the design uses large type;
- polishing a useful sentence until it no longer sounds spoken.

## The concrete-copy rule

Every important statement should contain at least one of the following:

1. a moment the couple recognizes;
2. an action Patrick takes;
3. a decision Patrick helps make;
4. an observable result;
5. a real object, room, venue, person, song, or constraint;
6. a fact that can be checked;
7. a direct statement of what is included or what happens next.

If a sentence contains none of these, it is probably atmosphere copy. Rewrite it or remove it.

### Abstract version

> Seamless planning creates a cohesive flow from ceremony through reception.

### Concrete direction

> We confirm the ceremony cues, speech microphones, first-dance version, and reception timing before the wedding.

### Abstract version

> I read the room and shape the energy in real time.

### Concrete direction

> I watch who is dancing, what they stay for, and what clears space. The next song comes from that.

### Abstract version

> An elevated soundtrack tailored to your unique story.

### Concrete direction

> Tell me the songs you love, the ones you cannot stand, and what your friends will sing before they dance.

The concrete versions are directional examples, not approved copy.

## Words that now require evidence

These words are not permanently banned. They are no longer allowed as shortcuts.

### Flow

Name what is moving and what Patrick does about it. Guest movement? Ceremony cues? Dinner ending? The start of dancing?

If the sentence works after deleting *flow*, delete it.

### Pacing

Name the timing decision. For example: speeches before dinner, how long formalities take, when the floor opens, or whether another slow song belongs there.

### Energy

Name the evidence. Are more people joining? Is the floor thinning? Are guests singing? Has the room had enough intensity?

### Atmosphere

Name what creates it: volume, lighting, familiar music, conversation, weather, the room, or the crowd.

### Arc

This is an internal planning word unless a customer genuinely understands something better because of it. On the website, the actual sequence is usually clearer.

### Intentional

Name the decision. If no decision can be named, the word is decoration.

### Seamless

Usually delete it. Describe the preparation or handoff that prevents a problem.

### Premium, elevated, curated, bespoke, unforgettable

Do not use these to describe Howe Sound Wedding DJ. Let photography, decisions, reviews, equipment, service details, and price posture carry the impression.

### Calm

Do not use *calm*, *calm planning*, *calm confidence*, or related language as a sales claim. Patrick has explicitly rejected it. Describe the useful action instead: quick replies, confirmed details, a tested microphone, a written plan, or a clear next step.

## Constructions to reject on sight

Unless Patrick has supplied the exact phrase from real speech, reject:

- `X isn't Y. It's Z.`
- `More than a...`
- `From X to Y...`
- `Where X meets Y.`
- `Your X. Your Y. Your Z.`
- `Designed to...`
- `Tailored to...`
- `Built around you.`
- `Every detail...`
- `At the heart of...`
- `A celebration as unique as...`
- `Your vision, brought to life.`
- `We don't just... We...`
- three balanced fragments presented as a profound idea;
- a question followed by an inspirational answer;
- a negative claim about other DJs used to make Patrick look better;
- a genre list standing in for musical judgment;
- an adjective pile standing in for evidence;
- a label such as `THE EXPERIENCE`, `THE JOURNEY`, or `OUR APPROACH` when a direct label would help more.

Also reject any sentence that could be moved unchanged to a photographer, planner, caterer, hotel, SaaS company, or another DJ's website.

## Headline standard

A headline has one job. It should not try to carry the whole brand.

A good HSDJ headline should do at least one of these:

- say what the section is about;
- make a useful promise that the section proves;
- express a real opinion;
- name a recognizable wedding moment;
- help the visitor decide whether to keep reading.

It should usually:

- be understandable on the first read;
- fit without broken words or forced line breaks;
- survive being read aloud;
- avoid a three-part construction;
- avoid sounding like a poster simply because it is set in large type;
- be shorter than the explanation below it;
- still mean something without the visual treatment.

Plain is allowed.

`What is included` may be better than a clever line that hides what is included.

## Paragraph standard

Each paragraph should make one useful point.

For service pages:

- lead with the answer;
- include the detail that makes the answer credible;
- say why it matters only if that is not already obvious;
- remove the final sentence if it merely restates the first two.

For editorial pages:

- begin with an observation, not a thesis about weddings;
- separate what Patrick has seen from what is general advice;
- do not invent a couple, event, weather condition, or crowd response;
- let venue detail carry the writing;
- make a useful point before linking to a service.

## Page-by-page voice roles

### Homepage

Job: make the right couple want to know more.

Use a small amount of memorable language, real wedding imagery, Patrick's point of view, and direct routes to Weddings, Reviews, and date checking. Do not explain the full service on the homepage.

### Weddings overview

Job: explain what Patrick actually handles across the day.

Use recognizable moments and service actions. This page should reduce uncertainty without reading like a technical manual. Do not repeat the homepage hero or borrow its scale for every heading.

### Packages

Job: help someone compare coverage and understand what changes between options.

Be especially plain here. No luxury tier names, artificial scarcity, or vague value language. If a feature needs interpretation, explain what it changes for the wedding.

### Reviews

Job: let previous couples answer the trust question.

Give the couple, the quote, and useful context room to work. Keep marketer commentary minimal. Do not convert every review into a slogan or a proof category.

### About

Job: let someone decide whether they want Patrick at their wedding.

Use first person. Include taste, experience, local connection, working habits, and a little personality. Do not write a founder mythology.

### Contact and FAQ

Job: remove hesitation and make the next step obvious.

Use the plainest voice on the site. Answer the question asked. Do not place a brand paragraph between the visitor and the answer.

### Venue and location pages

Job: prove useful familiarity with a real place and help a couple plan.

Publish only verified facts and clearly marked firsthand observations. If a page has no venue-specific knowledge beyond public marketing language, it is not ready. Local SEO is not a reason to publish interchangeable prose.

### Guides

Job: help a couple make a decision or avoid a problem.

Answer the question early. Use examples. Separate opinion from fact. Do not stretch one useful paragraph into an article for search length.

### Stories

Job: show Patrick's eye and ear through something that really happened or through a clearly labelled editorial observation.

Do not fabricate documentary detail. Do not turn a story into a disguised strategy essay. If the story could happen at any venue with any DJ, it needs more truth or should not be published.

### Metadata and search copy

Job: state accurately what the page contains and why it is relevant.

Search descriptions are not exempt from the voice standard. Do not insert *unforgettable experiences*, *seamless planning*, or similar language to fill character space.

## Truth and attribution rules

Every claim belongs to one of five source classes:

1. **Patrick firsthand:** Patrick personally did, saw, heard, or decided it.
2. **Customer firsthand:** a review, email, or interview that can be attributed.
3. **Venue verified:** confirmed by the venue's current official information or direct contact.
4. **Operational fact:** part of HSDJ's real service, equipment, pricing, process, or policy.
5. **Editorial opinion:** Patrick's clearly presented judgment, not disguised as universal fact.

If the source class is unknown, the claim does not go live.

Never:

- invent client details to make copy vivid;
- imply experience at a venue without evidence;
- turn a likely outcome into a guarantee;
- describe every wedding as a packed club night;
- claim all genres or all crowds;
- use a reviewer's words outside their meaning;
- borrow a fact from another DJ's site;
- publish a venue rule without checking whether it is current.

## The no-repetition rule

Each page and each section needs its own reason to exist.

Before writing, complete this sentence:

> This section exists so that a prospective client can ________.

Valid endings include:

- compare the packages;
- understand what ceremony sound includes;
- hear how Patrick thinks about requests;
- see evidence from previous couples;
- check whether their venue is in the service area;
- know what happens after an inquiry.

Invalid endings include:

- feel the brand;
- reinforce the experience;
- add visual interest;
- explain the approach again;
- improve SEO without adding useful information.

If two sections complete the sentence the same way, combine them or remove one.

## Directional rewrites from the current language system

These examples show how to move from strategy language toward useful speech. They are not approved production lines.

### Instead of

> Your story. Your energy. Your people.

Try the underlying truth:

> Tell me what you love and who is coming. We will start there.

### Instead of

> One cohesive sound and flow.

Try the actual distinction:

> The ceremony, dinner, and dance floor all need different things from the DJ.

### Instead of

> The same care in every tier.

Try the package fact:

> Every package includes planning, professional sound, and a proper backup plan.

Only use this if every package actually includes those things.

### Instead of

> A clear path beats guessing in the final weeks.

Try the customer answer:

> After you inquire, I will tell you what happens next and what I need from you.

### Instead of

> Bring the guide into your real timeline.

Try the action:

> Use the guide while you plan the music and timing for your venue.

### Instead of

> Professional planning from first contact to last song.

Try the work:

> We confirm the music, timing, announcements, and sound plan before the wedding.

## The source-first writing process

No major page should be rewritten from the current page alone. That causes old abstractions to be paraphrased instead of removed.

For each page:

### 1. Name the decision

What decision should this page help a prospective client make?

### 2. Gather source material

Use the smallest relevant set:

- Patrick interview notes;
- real emails with private information removed;
- consultation phrases Patrick uses naturally;
- verified service inclusions;
- customer reviews;
- venue facts;
- actual event stories;
- real photographs and artifacts.

### 3. Write the ugly version

State the facts in plain language. Do not write headings yet. Do not make it sound branded.

### 4. Find Patrick in it

Add the opinion, observation, humour, or phrasing that only Patrick would contribute. Do not add all four to every paragraph.

### 5. Remove strategy language

Circle every abstraction. Replace it with a moment, action, decision, fact, or consequence.

### 6. Run the competitor-swap test

Replace Howe Sound Wedding DJ with another supplier's name. If the passage still works unchanged, it is not specific enough.

### 7. Read it aloud

If Patrick would not say it, the first-person line fails. If a visitor needs a second read, the service line fails.

### 8. Check the design

No word may be cut off to preserve a composition. Do not add copy to fill a card. Do not create a card because copy exists. Text and composition must be resolved together.

### 9. Check mobile separately

Do not approve copy based only on desktop. Headline wraps, paragraph length, button language, and evidence order must work on a narrow screen without becoming a tall stack of promotional cards.

### 10. Get Patrick's approval

Generated or edited copy remains draft until Patrick has read it in context.

## Copy review scorecard

Mark each item **Yes**, **No**, or **Not applicable**.

### Truth

- Can every factual claim be sourced?
- Is firsthand knowledge distinguished from opinion?
- Are venue details current and verified?
- Is the copy honest about what Patrick controls?

### Usefulness

- Does the section help the visitor make one identifiable decision?
- Does it answer a question a couple might actually ask?
- Is there a concrete detail that makes the answer credible?
- Would the page lose something useful if the section disappeared?

### Voice

- Could Patrick say every first-person line naturally?
- Is the wording free of luxury-agency performance?
- Are contractions and sentence lengths natural?
- Does the copy allow a plain sentence to stay plain?
- Is humour earned rather than inserted?

### Anti-template

- Does the copy avoid the rejected constructions?
- Could it belong unchanged to another wedding supplier?
- Is the section doing something different from the section before it?
- Has strategy vocabulary been translated into customer language?
- Does the page avoid a repeated headline/blurb/three-card formula?

### Presentation

- Is every word visible at all supported screen sizes?
- Is the headline easy to read without relying on decoration?
- Does the amount of text match the importance of the section?
- Is the call to action a natural next step?
- Is the design helping the words rather than compensating for them?

### Conversion

- Does the section move the right person closer to a useful next action?
- Is that action clear?
- Has evidence appeared before a significant ask?
- Does the copy reduce a real doubt rather than create artificial urgency?

## Decision statuses

Every audited section receives one status:

- **KEEP:** useful, specific, sourced, and presented well.
- **TIGHTEN:** the job is right; excess language needs removal.
- **REWRITE FROM SOURCE:** the section is useful but the current language cannot be safely paraphrased.
- **PATRICK VOICE REQUIRED:** the section needs an opinion, story, or phrase that cannot be invented.
- **MERGE:** another section performs the same job.
- **REMOVE:** no distinct customer or business purpose survives the audit.
- **VERIFY:** a claim or venue detail needs a reliable source.
- **REDESIGN WITH COPY:** the content and its presentation are failing together.

## What this standard means for the current website

The next stage is not a page-by-page cosmetic rewrite and it is not another site-wide template pass.

The correct order is:

1. Freeze new promotional copy until the source and voice audit begins.
2. Build one inventory of all public-facing copy, including metadata and form states.
3. Assign each section a purpose, source class, decision status, and next action.
4. Start with the highest-conversion pages: Contact, Weddings, Packages, Reviews, and About.
5. Collect Patrick's missing voice material before rewriting first-person sections.
6. Rewrite from source, not from the old prose.
7. Recompose sections whose layout is forcing copy into cards, slogans, or broken wraps.
8. Audit venue pages separately for verified local knowledge and duplicated structures.
9. Audit Guides and Stories for usefulness, attribution, and invented editorial tone.
10. Return to the Homepage last, after the rest of the site can substantiate what it promises.

## Required Patrick source session

Before the major copy pass, record or write Patrick's unpolished answers to these prompts:

1. What do couples most often misunderstand about what a wedding DJ does?
2. What are the first three things you listen for when the dance floor opens?
3. Tell the story of a song choice that changed because of the room.
4. What makes dinner music work without becoming wallpaper?
5. What do you check before anybody gives a speech?
6. What is one wedding-DJ habit you never want to copy?
7. When do requests help, and when do you ignore one?
8. What is different about working in Squamish or the Sea-to-Sky corridor?
9. What do you want a couple to tell you before you discuss genres?
10. What is the nicest thing a couple has said that felt accurate rather than flattering?
11. What part of the job looks easy only because you prepared for it?
12. What should a couple know before deciding whether you are their DJ?

Do not clean up Patrick's answers while collecting them. The phrases, detours, and specific nouns are the useful material.
