## Decision

Patrick's answers are useful precisely because they are not campaign copy. They explain what he actually asks, hears, checks, and changes. The site should be rebuilt around those actions and the client's decisions, not around another set of polished slogans.

Do **not** paste the answers wholesale onto the website. Do **not** treat this interview as consent to publish every anecdote. Do **not** treat the draft V2 voice standard as approved; its marker is still pending. Do **not** change a live page or composition under the Anti-Template Protocol without the required mission-specific creative approval.

The immediate authorized work is research: establish the source, identify where it changes the existing story, audit current copy, and prepare one bounded first mission for Patrick's review.

## The business story the answers actually support

### Couple control and DJ judgment coexist

Patrick does not demand a blank slate. Couples choose the important ceremony, entrance, and first-dance songs and may choose plenty of dance-party music too. A long playlist is welcome as a reference, not a burden or a script he must play in order. Patrick mixes, selects, and responds to the room around that input. This is a more compelling proposition than the site's repeated “not a generic playlist” line because it resolves a real buyer question.

**Source:** Q1, Q7, Q9.  
**Potential visitor question:** “Can we choose the music, or does the DJ decide?”  
**Caution:** “I play about 2.5 minutes of most songs” is Patrick's typical practice, not a fixed promise for every song. Some songs should play longer; formal songs are not subject to an automatic cut. Do not suggest every requested song will be played or that the couple's preferences can be overridden casually.

### He listens to more than the dance floor

When dancing begins, Patrick notices requests, cheering and laughter, and whether the room's level and tonal balance feel right. This joins music choice to actual sound work. It is more distinctive than saying he “reads the room” without naming what he is reading.

**Source:** Q2.  
**Potential visitor question:** “What does a live DJ actually do once the party starts?”  
**Caution:** Do not reduce every decision to BPM matching; meaningful requests can work for social reasons, and not every song choice has an obvious measurable signal.

### A curveball can change the floor

Patrick describes a left-field rock song suddenly making heads turn and bringing people onto the dance floor. That is a useful observation about surprise, recognition, and a DJ's willingness to follow a response he can see. It belongs beside his description of listening to the room, not inside a fabricated wedding narrative.

**Source:** updated Q3.  
**Potential visitor question:** “Will the DJ stick rigidly to one sound if our crowd wants something else?”  
**Caution:** Q3 is complete as an observation, but it still does not identify a particular song, couple, venue, or event. The word *always* is Patrick's conversational emphasis, not a promise that rock invariably fills a floor.

### Dinner is part of the musical night, not wallpaper

Patrick prefers upbeat classics that make people move a little while they eat. He may build the tempo, intensity, and volume over dinner so the dance floor does not begin from a dead stop.

**Source:** Q4.  
**Potential visitor question:** “Will the music before dancing sound like us?”  
**Caution:** This is a working preference to discuss with a couple, not a universal promise to raise dinner volume at every wedding. Conversation, speeches, venue constraints, and the couple's brief can change the choice.

### The unnoticed work has names

Before a speech, Patrick checks for the coordinator's go-ahead, photographer and videographer readiness, the wireless microphone signal, and the speaker's readiness. This is a credible service example. It also shows that the job is collaborative; do not imply Patrick alone controls the timeline or other vendors.

**Source:** Q5.  
**Potential visitor question:** “Who makes sure the speech actually starts properly?”

### Local knowledge is operational, not scenic

Patrick knows recurring vendors and prepares for outdoor sites, power limitations, wind, and isolated rain. The site should name these realities where relevant and verified, not keep asserting that he “knows the corridor.”

**Source:** Q8.  
**Potential visitor question:** “What changes when a wedding is outside or at a Sea-to-Sky venue?”  
**Caution:** Q8 does not establish firsthand experience at every listed venue or that every venue has the same power/weather constraints. Verify specific venue claims individually.

### A request may carry a whole group with it

Patrick values a request when it has shared history or emotional weight and when it can work musically in the moment. He may suggest another song if he does not know or have it, or if it would clear the floor. This is a useful explanation of judgment, not a public “I ignore requests” posture.

**Source:** Q7.  
**Potential visitor question:** “What happens when our guests request songs?”

### Musical breadth is an experience claim, not a genre pile

Patrick works beyond weddings, including private and club events, and does not rely on a small, standard wedding rotation. He prepares across styles so he can stay with a crowd that responds to country, Latin music, rock, or something else. That is more credible than listing every genre in one sales sentence.

**Source:** Q11 and Q12.  
**Potential visitor question:** “Can he play music that is actually ours?”  
**Caution:** “Know all genres” and “deep into each genre” are conversational emphasis, not defensible literal guarantees of every culture, genre, or subgenre. Verify any named events or professional credits before using them as third-party proof.

### Care is best shown through conduct

The praise Patrick most believes is that he genuinely cared about a couple and their celebration. The site should prove that through decisions, preparation, and attributable reviews rather than declare “I care” repeatedly.

**Source:** Q10.  
**Caution:** Q10 paraphrases a couple's feedback. Do not format it as a direct review quotation without locating the original text and permission/context.

### His microphone personality has a boundary

Patrick does not want clichéd DJ patter during the dance party. That is useful positioning if stated plainly, but should not become a hostile caricature of other DJs.

**Source:** Q6.  
**Caution:** Patrick's source wording can stay in this research record. Public copy need not repeat the profanity to prove authenticity.

## The story boundary and the sensitive trade-off

### Q3 is an observation, not a documented case study

Patrick completed Q3 on 2026-09-17. His answer describes curveball rock songs that make guests turn toward the music and rush the dance floor. It does not name one particular event. We can use the observation as Patrick's musical judgment after his review, but we cannot turn it into a named event story or claim that every left-field rock selection gets that result. A real case study would still require the song, setting, what Patrick played before and after, and permission to publish any identifiable detail.

### Q9 needs a couple-first formulation

Patrick's Northern Alberta example describes a genuine tension: a couple's playlist may not represent their guests' shared music. It does **not** license copy that says a couple's genre choices “don't matter.” The couple decides what they want to hear and how much guest response they want to prioritize; Patrick can explain the trade-off, ask about their guests, and respond within the agreed boundaries.

The anecdote is useful internally. Before publication, confirm Patrick is comfortable with it, remove identifying event details as needed, and make sure it does not read as “I know better than my clients.”

## First high-intent copy inventory

This is a **triage inventory**, not approval of proposed replacement copy. File references identify the source as inspected on 2026-09-17; the working tree already contains unrelated user changes. Each section still needs the full section contract and desktop/mobile composition mission before production change.

### Contact — first mission

**Visitor decision:** “How do I find out if Patrick is available and what happens next?”

- **KEEP / VERIFY:** date, venue, partial details being acceptable, Patrick replying personally, and the consult route. Verify consultation duration and actual response promise against operations.
- **MERGE:** the opening promise, “What happens after you submit,” “Partial details are still a useful start,” and “Why couples reach out early” repeat the same reassurance in `src/app/contact/page.tsx`. Choose one concise explanation at the point of action.
- **REDESIGN WITH COPY:** the page exposes Book a Consult, availability form, separate email form, action trio, and more next-step copy. Determine which action is primary for a visitor with a date versus one without; do not remove a functional path without confirming the actual booking workflow.
- **REWRITE FROM SOURCE:** “align on music, flow, and coverage” and “A clear path beats guessing” should answer concrete process questions. Q1 and Q9 suggest better consult prompts: key songs, how the couple likes to dance, and what guests respond to.
- **VERIFY:** current metadata calls the consult a complimentary 45-minute meeting. Confirm before retaining this exact operational claim.

**Contact implementation finding:** `src/config/site-scheduling.ts` currently defines one public Calendly event called Sound Check, a 45-minute duration, and “Book a Consult” as the public button label. This verifies the site's *configuration*, not that the external appointment is still configured that way. The date form has distinct available, unavailable, and manual-review states; an available date leads to a consult invitation with an optional inquiry fallback. The separate message form exists for people not ready to check a date. Preserve these state paths while simplifying their presentation.

**Specific repetition:** `src/components/contact-book-consult-section.tsx` repeats “No pressure,” “No commitment,” and “No awkward sales pitch” in one list, after a “45 minutes · No pressure · Just clarity” line. `src/components/contact-availability-form.tsx` then repeats links back to consult and message above the date field. The visitor needs a clear choice once, then a clear next step in the appropriate result state. Repetition is not additional reassurance.

**Recommended information hierarchy for the composition mission (not approved layout):** lead with the consult as the direct conversation path; show date-check as the equally findable alternative for a visitor who knows their date; keep a plain message path for someone with incomplete details or a vendor inquiry. Once a route is selected, do not keep selling all three routes at each step. The availability result should determine the next action without suggesting an unconfirmed booking.

### Weddings overview — second mission

**Visitor decision:** “Does Patrick cover the parts of our wedding that matter, and will the music be ours?”

- **KEEP / EXPAND FROM SOURCE:** ceremony support, microphone readiness, dinner music, live mixing, and music planning. Q1, Q4, and Q5 supply unusually concrete detail.

The copy should resolve the playlist-control question and distinguish chosen formal songs from live reception choices. Q3 adds a concrete reason not to lock the reception to one sound: sometimes the unexpected rock track is the one guests run toward.

- **PATRICK VOICE REQUIRED:** how he asks a couple to balance their taste with the songs their guests love. Q9 is a beginning, not final customer-facing copy.

## The first bounded mission: Contact decision path

**Single job:** A prospective client can check a date or book a consult and understand what Patrick needs from them and what happens after submitting.

**Why first:** It is the point where interest becomes an inquiry. Redundant choices and reassurances can obstruct action. It can be simplified without requiring an invented event story.

**Required pre-code work:**

1. Verify the real operational path: date-check result, consult booking, fallback inquiry, reply owner and timing, 45-minute duration, and incomplete-detail handling.
2. Inventory all Contact copy including child components, success/error states, and metadata.
3. Keep the actual form and booking behavior intact; propose a simpler hierarchy around them.
4. Produce distinct desktop and mobile static compositions in the designated research area. Identify the nearest conventional contact-page pattern, its physical HSDJ ancestor, and how the design avoids copying either the homepage or a generic mixer panel.
5. Run Anti-Design and Conversion Guardian critiques, then Patrick's taste review.
6. Only after the mission record contains the exact viewport-specific `PATRICK_CREATIVE_APPROVAL: GRANTED` marker may production implementation start.
7. Test the approved path end-to-end, including keyboard, mobile, form errors, confirmation, and analytics.

**Copy prompt for Patrick at that gate:** “What do you actually write back to someone who sends a date but does not know their venue yet?” His real response is a better source than a generic promise of clarity.
